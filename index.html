Job Market Paper

Global Financial Integration and Productivity: The Role of Risk Sharing

with Takaaki Sagawa

[Draft coming soon!]

Abstract

This paper studies how integration into global capital markets affects aggregate productivity through risk sharing and the allocation of capital. We develop a small open economy model where heterogeneous capitalists choose between producing and investing. Foreign participation in domestic equity markets shifts the country’s aggregate risk abroad and lowers the hurdle rate for production. This raises investment and output but lowers aggregate TFP by drawing less productive capitalists into production. Greater domestic financing capacity instead raises TFP by allowing productive firms to expand and displace marginal producers. We validate these predictions using emerging-market equity liberalizations in the late 1980s and 1990s, which were followed by equity price booms, more listings, and slower TFP growth where booms were larger. The calibrated model shows that liberalization alone lowers TFP and raises output only temporarily, while liberalization with deeper domestic financing raises both.

Working Papers

Revisiting the Global Allocation Puzzle: The Composition of Capital Flows

with Takaaki Sagawa

[Draft coming soon!]

Abstract

This paper studies the composition of cross-border capital flows by distinguishing between debt and equity assets. We show that equity inflows are positively associated with total factor productivity (TFP) growth, consistent with neoclassical theory, whereas debt inflows are negatively associated with TFP growth. The same divergence appears in the cross-section of net external positions, with fiscal capacity serving as the key country fundamental associated with these patterns. Motivated by this evidence, we develop a multi-country model with heterogeneous entrepreneurs, financial constraints, and international portfolio choice to explain the joint determination of TFP and the composition of global capital flows. Differences in fiscal capacity alter both the supply of government bonds and the risk-adjusted return on domestic risky assets, producing the opposing flows of debt and equity observed in the data.

Generalist Inventors and Induced Innovation

with Lior Shabtai (co-author’s job market paper)

[Draft coming soon!]

Abstract

This paper provides evidence that workers with broader experience play a key role in firms’ response to changing investment opportunities. Using patent records on 68,000 inventors at U.S. manufacturing firms (1992–2021) and a reduced-form shift-share design, we find that broader inventors increase green patenting more than narrower inventors following energy-cost shocks that raise firms’ returns to green innovation. The induced green patents are more likely to involve new collaborators and are less scientifically important than these inventors’ usual green patents. Team characteristics do not predict the quality decline, which is also not explained by inventors’ prior green experience, observed ability, cross-firm mobility, or technological distance from prior work. These findings suggest that firms assign versatile inventors to newly viable projects of lower scientific value.

Work in Progress

  • Risk Sharing and Credit Allocation in Bank Networks with Anna Rogantini-Picco
    Abstract

    We study how regional contractions spill over to bank credit supply through banking groups. Using euro-area bank-level data, we exploit the 2022 euro-area energy price surge as a plausibly exogenous shock, whose regional shock intensity was shaped by pre-existing industrial composition. Our empirical design isolates credit-supply spillovers from local demand by comparing banks within the same region whose sibling banks elsewhere in the group were differentially exposed to the energy-price shock. We document a contraction in credit supply driven by sibling exposure: a one-standard-deviation increase in sibling exposure raises new mortgage spreads by about 7 basis points and reduces new mortgage origination by about 10 percent. Firm revolving-credit spreads also rise with volumes unchanged.

  • Fiscal Stress and Macroeconomic Fragility with Brian Livingston
    Abstract

    We study whether sovereign fiscal stress amplifies the effects of macroeconomic shocks. Using sovereign spreads relative to the German bund to assess fiscal stress, we observe that in response to oil shocks, carbon policy shocks, and three different monetary policy shocks, real and financial variables deteriorate by more for Eurozone economies during periods of high fiscal stress than during normal times. Moreover, while Federal Reserve and BoE monetary policy shocks lead to worse stock returns for more export-facing firms in normal times, under fiscal stress this relative performance mostly reverses, suggesting domestic amplification.
